National Parks

The great national parks preserve far more than scenery. They protect raw chapters of Earth history — canyon walls cut through vanished seas, volcanoes that rebuilt entire landscapes, desert towers carved grain by grain, glaciers that quarried mountains, faults that lifted ranges, and fossils left behind by worlds that no longer exist.

Protected wilderness. Exposed time.

Where beauty and deep time meet in the open air.

National parks are among the rare places where nature is allowed to remain large enough to reveal its own logic. Cliffs weather where they stand. Rivers keep cutting. Dunes migrate. Ice advances and retreats. Forests return after fire. Rockfalls expose fresh stone. The landscape is not a backdrop — it is an active record of the planet at work.

Protection matters because geology has context. A slab of sandstone in a collection is interesting; a thousand-foot wall of that sandstone, still resting on the beds beneath it and still being sculpted by wind, frost, gravity, and water, is a story. Wilderness preserves the relationships between rock, landform, water, climate, plants, animals, and time.

Beneath Our Feet approaches each park as both a place of wonder and a piece of evidence. We begin with what you can see, then move backward through the events that made it possible.

Look longer. Look deeper.

The view is only the beginning.

A canyon rim may stop you with its beauty. A volcanic basin may feel almost otherworldly. A granite dome, a fossil bed, a desert arch, or a glacier may seem permanent. None of them are. The deeper pleasure is discovering the sequence of events — often spanning hundreds of millions of years — that assembled the scene in front of you.

These protected places give us something increasingly rare: enough intact landscape to see the evidence at full scale. They are wilderness, refuge, beauty, and raw rock — but they are also pages of the oldest history book we will ever read.
